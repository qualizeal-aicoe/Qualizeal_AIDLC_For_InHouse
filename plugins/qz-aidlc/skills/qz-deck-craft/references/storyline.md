# Storyline · the answer first, then why it is safe to say yes

## Start from the room
| Ask | Because |
|---|---|
| Who decides, and who can say no? | you write for the decider and prepare for the blocker |
| What do they already believe? | you cannot spend slides on what they know, or skip what they doubt |
| What do they do the minute after the meeting? | that is your closing slide |
| What would make them look bad for saying yes? | that is the risk you must take off the table |
| How long, really, once introductions are done? | plan to finish in two thirds of the slot |

## The governing message
One sentence: the recommendation and the reason. "Approve a sixteen-week pilot on two teams: every decision stays
with a named person, and you will see a real story delivered in week three." Not a topic ("AI-DLC overview"), and
not a hope ("transform delivery").

Under it, three supporting points at most, each of which a sceptic could check. If you have five, two of them are
details of the others.

## Order
Lead with the answer. Then earn it:
1. **Their situation**, in their words and numbers. Two slides at most. They decide here whether this is about them.
2. **What it costs them** to leave it as it is.
3. **The answer**: what we will build, in one picture.
4. **Why it works**: how it runs, what keeps it safe, who stays in control.
5. **Proof**: what is tested today, and what we will confirm on their estate.
6. **What it takes**: plan, team, commercials.
7. **The ask**: the decision, and the next three steps with an owner and a date each.

An executive audience reads the first three slides and the last one. Those four must work alone.

## Titles
A title is the sentence you would say if the projector failed.
- A claim, with a verb or a number: "Four agents, each with a defined job and limits". Not "Agent architecture".
- One line: under about 54 characters in the QualiZeal template.
- No questions. Answer them.
- No two slides with the same title. If you need "(continued)", the slide is two points.
- The subtitle states the so-what, or is left out. It is not a second title.

**The read-through test.** Read only the titles, in order, to someone outside the project. If they can tell you the
recommendation and the reasons, the deck works. If they ask "so what?" at any title, that slide is a label.
`deck_review` prints the titles this way for exactly this.

## One slide, one point
If a slide needs "and" in its title, split it. If a point needs no evidence, it is a sentence on another slide. Cut
any slide the argument survives without, and keep it as a backup.

Time: about two minutes a slide. Thirty minutes is ten to twelve slides and time to talk. More slides than minutes
is a document: send it as one.

## Numbers and claims
Every figure says how it was obtained.
| Label | Means |
|---|---|
| MEASURED | we ran it and counted; say where and when |
| ESTIMATED | calculated from stated inputs; say which |
| ASSUMED | a planning value to be replaced; say by when |
| TARGET | what we commit to aim for, agreed with them |

Put the label or the source on the slide (`"source"` in the spec), not only in the notes. Round honestly: "about a
third" is better than "34.7%" from a sample of twenty. Never show a number from another client.

Two columns keep you honest and are more persuasive than certainty: **tested today** and **to confirm with you**.

## Opening and closing
Open on their problem, in a sentence they would say themselves. Not on our company, our history or the agenda.

Close on the ask. The last slide names the decision and the next steps, each with a date and an owner on both
sides. "Questions?" and "Thank you" are not closing slides.

## Notes
For each slide: the point in one sentence, the proof you will point at, and the line that takes you to the next
slide. Written so that a colleague could present it tomorrow. Anything said aloud that is not on the slide and not
in the notes will be lost.
